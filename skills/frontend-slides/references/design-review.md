# Content and design review

Read this before placing images and again before delivering. Overflow, navigation, and export checks do not cover anything here.

## 1. Image truth

For every image, open the file and describe what is visible, including what is absent: "the back of a remote control with the battery cover open; no buttons are visible."

Then test:

- Does the slide need this image to show something? Does it show that thing?
- Would a viewer read the intended meaning without the caption? If the image needs the caption to become true, it is decoration or evidence for a different claim.
- Does the caption name what is in the image? Does the alt text describe the image rather than restate the slide's claim?

Write alt text and captions from the image you see, never from the argument you want. If the image is wrong, replace it, change the claim, or remove it; do not keep it because it is the only asset or because the path loads.

Check license and credit when the image came from outside the supplied material.

If you cannot view images, hand the check to an agent with vision or ask the user to confirm. If neither is available, report the image check as unverified. Do not infer content from filenames, alt text, search snippets, or dimensions, and do not report the check as passed.

Programmatic inspection — file type, dimensions, palette, OCR, or blob counts — is not semantic vision. It can tell you an image is suspicious; it cannot tell you what the image shows. Use it to raise a question, never to pass the check.

When you delegate, pass the claim and the caption, and ask the delegate to state what the image shows and whether it supports the claim. A generic "does this look good" pass checks balance and spacing, not truth.

A file loading and the layout staying clean say nothing about whether the image supports the point. A photo of a remote's battery compartment under "too many buttons" passes every technical check and fails the argument.

## 2. Claims and examples

- Trace every factual sentence to the supplied material or a cited source. If the material does not support a detail, cut the detail or mark it as an inference.
- An example must demonstrate the claim it sits beside. An unrelated anecdote or a near-miss weakens the slide even when it looks good.
- Prefer one accurate example to three decorative ones.
- Verify or remove quotes, numbers, dates, names, and attributions.
- Mark opinion as opinion. Do not turn a plausible explanation into a fact because it makes the slide cleaner.

## 3. Page composition

A slide is not a container for everything known about a topic.

- Give each slide one job: establish, explain, compare, demonstrate, or close.
- Name the first thing the audience should see, then the second. If two elements compete for first, re-rank or split.
- A title can carry the claim, or it can name the section. A title should do one of those jobs; it should not sit above five elements that all compete for attention.
- Choose one dominant element per slide — a headline, an image, a number, a diagram, or a comparison pair — and keep the supporting text supporting.
- When title, claim, explanation, image, speaker prompt, and conclusion all appear, move the speaker prompt to notes and split or cut the rest.
- Use negative space to protect the focal point. Do not fill it with chrome.

## 4. Decoration audit

"AI-looking" usually means generic template grammar applied without a content reason. It does not mean the deck is too plain, too bold, too minimal, or too Swiss.

For every repeated element — outer border, card frame, mono eyebrow, principle number, chip, timeline, highlight, icon — name the job it does for this deck. If the honest answer is "it looked designed" or "the template had it", remove it or replace it with something the content needs.

Audit these patterns:

- stacked frames around every page, and frames inside frames;
- labels or numbers that repeat the title instead of adding information;
- a highlight color used on every page until it stops meaning anything;
- three equal cards used for content that is not three parallel items;
- icons that illustrate the label rather than the argument;
- a timeline whose dates do not change the decision;
- a picture on every page because the layout expects one.

The fix is subtraction and re-ranking, not a different decoration. Cards, borders, mono labels, and grids are legitimate when they carry the structure. Minimal is not automatically better; the test is whether the element serves this content and the chosen direction.

Do not reject a style because it is common. Swiss, editorial, brutalist, and minimal decks can all be good. Judge the execution against the brief, not against a fashionable palette.

## 5. Image-text composition

- Put the image where the argument needs it, not in the space that is left.
- Crop for the subject; keep faces, hands, and key objects out of the crop.
- Align the image to the grid and to the type. Optical alignment beats mathematical centering for circles, arrows, and asymmetric shapes.
- Keep the caption with the image. Alt text describes the image; the caption may add context or credit.
- Match the mat or background to the deck surface; a white photo rectangle on an off-white page reads as a mistake.
- One image, one job. Do not reuse a screenshot to decorate several slides.
- Leave breathing room around an image that carries the point. A photo pressed against a border reads as filler.

## 6. Whole-deck review

- The opening gives the audience a reason to care — a question, a claim, or a stake — not just a topic label.
- The visual system is recognisable across slide types: same grid, type scale, color roles, and image treatment.
- Pacing alternates dense and quiet pages. A deck of identical dense pages is exhausting; a deck of identical sparse pages says nothing.
- The ending makes one move: a decision, a question, a single line, or an action. Do not stack summary, quote, call to action, thanks, and credits.
- Read the deck in sequence, not only slide by slide. A good slide can be wrong in context.

## 7. Audience and speaker layers

Visible slide content belongs to the audience. Talk track, timing, "ask the room" prompts, and reminders belong in HTML comments, a notes file, or a presenter view. They may appear on the slide only when the user explicitly wants a teaching or facilitation deck that shows them. When in doubt, keep them off the surface.

## 8. When the user says it feels generic

1. Look at the rendered deck in sequence.
2. Name the concrete causes: which repeated elements, which competing focal points, which images do not support their claims, which pages have no job.
3. Propose a small set of structural changes: split, cut, re-rank, replace an image, change one type or color decision.
4. Re-render and compare before and after.
5. If the direction itself is wrong, return to step 3 and offer alternatives. Do not restyle by accumulating effects.

Do not answer a generic-feel complaint with pixel nudges, more decoration, or a new template applied over the same structure.
