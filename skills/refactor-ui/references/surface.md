# Surface: what belongs here

A surface is what the audience sees at once. Every element on it competes for the same attention. Most "too complex" complaints are too many simultaneous jobs, not too many pixels.

Read this file when the complaint is about amount, density, clarity of purpose, repetition, or interaction prominence.

## 1. Name the surface's job

Write the job as one sentence: understand a claim, make a decision, complete a task, compare options, feel a moment. A job is not "show everything about the topic".

- One surface carries one primary job. When two jobs are genuinely equal, split the surface or sequence them.
- If the medium cannot split — a modal, a single screen, a slide — keep the job that serves the user's next action and move the other down a layer.
- Different surfaces may carry different jobs. Do not force one job across a whole artifact.

Test: if you removed everything except what the job needs, what would remain? That remainder is the candidate core.

## 2. Place content on a layer

Content has four usable layers:

1. **Primary** — the claim, the decision, or the action the surface exists for.
2. **Support** — the one or two things needed to trust or act on the primary.
3. **On demand** — detail, definitions, edge cases, history, settings, secondary actions. Use a tooltip, expander, detail view, secondary panel, appendix, or notes file.
4. **Off-surface** — talk track, timing, facilitation prompts, implementation notes, and reminders.

Move content down the ladder before deleting it. Delete only content with no job at any layer.

- **Talk or presentation.** The audience watches and listens. The surface carries the claim; the speaker carries the explanation. Move full explanations and "ask the room" prompts to notes unless the deck is explicitly a self-reading handout.
- **Reading document.** The reader has no speaker. Keep the explanation, but give it hierarchy and grouping.
- **App or tool.** Prominence follows frequency and consequence. Low-frequency settings and destructive actions can move behind a menu — as long as a visible path remains.
- **Reference material.** Density is the point. Keep the data; fix grouping, alignment, and scan paths instead.

## 3. Run the repetition test

Two blocks with the same job compete. Merge them or remove one.

Common forms:

- a title and a takeaway saying the same thing in different words;
- a label that names a category the heading already makes obvious;
- an explanation paragraph that restates the claim;
- a closing summary that repeats the surface the viewer just saw;
- instructions that exist only because the design is unclear — fix the design, then cut the instruction.

Repetition can also be narrative work: an opening question answered at the end, a refrain, a recap that is itself the point. Keep repetition that pays off across time or sequence; cut repetition that competes inside one view.

Test: cover the candidate block. Does the surface still make the same point? If yes, the block is either a different job or removable. If no, it carries meaning — keep it and quiet it instead.

Optional is not competing. A subtitle, caption, byline, or annotation with a distinct job stays even when it could be removed. Do not use a global word-count target or cut ratio; a surface that is already quiet is not improved by becoming quieter.

Recaps are repetition when they restate claims the artifact already made. They are new content when they add a synthesis, a decision, or a consequence. A closing page that only repeats earlier pages can be cut; a section that turns the repetition into a conclusion is an argument, not a recap.

## 4. Edit copy without rewriting the argument

- Merge sentences that do the same job.
- Turn a paragraph into a claim plus one support.
- Cut setup, hedging, and meta-commentary ("In this slide we will…").
- Keep the author's voice, facts, qualifications, and meaning. Mark inference; do not invent a cleaner fact.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs. Do not turn every line into a slogan; slogans reduce precision.
- Credits, licences, and legal text are mandatory content. Keep them and give them a quiet, stable style.

Deleting an independent argument or changing a claim is a meaning change: confirm it. Compression and migration are not.

## 5. Density is not complexity

Dense can be correct: tables, dashboards, reference docs, schedules, data-rich screens, detailed reading material.

- Complexity is the number of competing decisions, not the number of pixels.
- Fix density with grouping, alignment, hierarchy, and progressive disclosure. Do not shrink type below comfortable reading, and do not hide data the user needs.
- If content still overflows, split the surface or move detail down a layer. Shrinking to fit is not a layout.
- A sparse surface that lacks enough information to act is also a failure. Empty is not the goal.

When a dense surface is also busy, keep the data and change the container:

- Group metrics or rows into two or three bands with a label or a clear spatial break. Keep every value.
- Merge repeated containers: one panel with dividers instead of N bordered cards; one filter row instead of a boxed toolbar.
- Spend the accent once — the primary action or the one alert — not on every sparkline, badge, and icon.
- Quiet metadata with weight and color before removing it. Labels and deltas can recede without disappearing.
- Set the scan path: the largest number, the active incident, or the primary action should win the first read.

## 6. Handle interaction prominence

When the complaint is about use rather than looks:

- Reproduce the task and find where the user hesitates, misreads, or backtracks. Watch the actual path, not the screenshot alone.
- Keep the primary action visible and unmistakable. Quiet secondary actions without hiding them.
- Progressive disclosure needs a visible entry point and a discoverable label. A hidden feature is not a simplification.
- Preserve keyboard access, focus states, labels, error messages, undo, and recovery.
- A flow problem is not a styling problem. Change the sequence, the default, or the affordance — not only its color.

## 7. Completion checks

- A new viewer can state the main point or the next action.
- Every visible block has a distinct job; no two blocks do the same job in the same view.
- Every moved or removed block is accounted for at another layer or intentionally gone.
- Dense surfaces remain scannable; sparse surfaces still carry enough to act.
- No required fact, instruction, credit, or capability disappeared.

## Avoid

- Cutting a uniform percentage from every surface.
- Shrinking type to make content fit.
- Hiding core features to look simple.
- Removing credits or qualifications because they are visually inconvenient.
- Applying "one idea per page" to material that is meant to be read in full.
- Replacing an explanation with a vague slogan.
